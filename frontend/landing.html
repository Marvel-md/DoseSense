THE MEASURE IN USE TODAY

Hospitals count what was collected.
Not what was taken.

Proportion of Days Covered reads the pharmacy record. It is the standard, it is computed everywhere, and it has two blind spots that no threshold can close.

BLIND SPOT ONE

Collecting is not swallowing

Someone can pick up every prescription on time, let the boxes gather in a drawer, and score a flawless 1.00. That patient is invisible to the metric entirely.

BLIND SPOT TWO

One threshold for everybody

A month-long gap is unremarkable for a patient who has always run late, and serious for one who never has. A population cut-off cannot tell a holiday from a decline.

WHAT WE LOOK FOR

Five signals drift.
One stays perfect.

Watch the lines behind this text. Symptoms rise, laboratory values move, attendance slips, activity falls — while the pharmacy record runs flat and clean.

That gap between what the pharmacy says and what the body says is the whole detection. It is the fingerprint of a medicine collected and not taken, and it is the one thing the conventional measure can never show.

HOW IT REASONS

Against the patient's own history.
Never against an average.

01 — BASELINE

Six routine records

Pharmacy collections, prescription changes, attendance, symptoms, laboratory results and activity. Each compared against how this person normally behaves.

02 — CHANGE

Did it come back?

A change point is located, then tested for reversion. A holiday is not a decline, and the system says so instead of raising an alert somebody has to dismiss.

03 — RESTRAINT

It abstains

Where the record is too thin to judge, it declines to give a number rather than producing one that looks like knowledge.
